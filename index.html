First ▸ what each machine actually is

Know your two machines

Both manuals are now the source for this. The short version: the Halo+ is a dry-heat machine that tumbles, and the Sakura is the wet machine that simmers unattended. Sending a dish to the wrong one is the main way this kind of cooking goes wrong.

From the manuals ▸ worth knowing

Nine things the manuals tell you

Small rules that save a mess

Never cook oats in milk in the Sakura. It boils over. Cook them in water on the porridge setting and stir the milk in at the end, once it has switched to keep-warm.

Nothing metal in the ceramic bowl, and no vinegar. Both damage the coating, and neither is covered by the warranty. Wooden or silicone only. Don't rinse rice in the bowl either — use a sieve.

Return the Halo to level before opening the lid. Tilted, the lid won't stay open on its own and can drop shut.

Sakura slow cooking is twice as fast as you expect. The Sakura is sealed, so a recipe written for 7–8 hours in a normal slow cooker is done in 3–4 hours here.

Don't steam anything thicker than 3.5 cm. And if you're steaming above rice at the same time, three cups of rice is the maximum or the basket hits the lid.

Frozen food needs no oil in the Halo at all. Only fresh chips and vegetables do, and then only half a measure for 800 g.

Breville advises against very fatty cuts. Skin-on chicken portions, lamb chops and fatty meat are specifically not recommended — the fat spatters onto the halogen assembly. Lean sausages and bacon on the rack are fine and in their own table.

Delayed start has minimums. Rice, porridge and soup need 70 minutes' notice, slow cook needs 130, yoghurt needs about six hours. Set it shorter and it just starts immediately.

Loosen rice the moment it finishes. Stirring it through with the plastic spatula lets excess steam out and is the difference between fluffy and claggy. Keep-warm runs 24 hours but rice goes off after about 15.

Reference ▸ seasoned rice pouches

Takikomi gohan, thirty-four kinds

炊き込みご飯の素 · takikomi gohan no moto · seasoned rice base. A pouch of seasoned liquid and pre-cooked ingredients that goes into the Sakura with rice and water — no extra water, since the pouch counts — and forty minutes later is a complete meal. Each pouch is sized for two or three cups; the packet says which. They are light and shelf-stable, so buy a dozen at a time online. Using them well

Do not add extra water. The pouch liquid is part of the measurement — rice in, pouch in, then water to the normal line for that number of cups and no further.

Match the pouch to the pot. Most are sized for two or three cups. Using a three-cup pouch on two cups of rice gives you something aggressively salty.

Brands to look for: Nagatanien (永谷園), Marumiya (丸美屋), Mizkan (ミツカン), Ajinomoto (味の素) and Daisho (ダイショー). Nagatanien and Marumiya cover most of the list above.

Where: WASO, Starry Mart, TK Trading, Atari-Ya and Natural Natural all carry a rotating selection far cheaper than Japan Centre, and Amazon marketplace multipacks are often cheapest per pouch. Search the Japanese characters rather than the English if a site's search is unhelpful.

Buying furikake well

Brands: Marumiya (丸美屋) for noritama and sukiyaki; Nagatanien (永谷園) for the Otona no Furikake sachet range; Mishima (三島食品) for yukari, which is the definitive version; Urashima (浦島) and Futaba (フタバ) for the fish-based blends.

The value buy is a multi-flavour assortment box — Marumiya's mini-pack assortments give you five or six flavours in single-serve sachets for the price of one shaker, which is the fastest way to find out what you actually like.

Beyond rice: on buttered toast, over a fried egg, through mashed avocado, shaken over popcorn, on roasted vegetables from the Halo, or stirred into plain noodles with butter. Aonori and gomashio in particular work on almost anything.

Keep it sealed. The seaweed goes limp within a fortnight of opening in a damp kitchen. Clip the bag or decant the shaker's contents into a jar.

Method ▸ still not cooking

Seven things that aren't cooking but act like it

None takes more than about ninety seconds, and each raises the ceiling on everything else here.

The seven

Finish with acid. A squeeze of lemon, a splash of vinegar or a spoon of pickle juice at the very end. This is the difference between food that tastes flat and food that tastes finished, and it costs nothing.

Finish with crunch. Crispy fried onions, toasted sesame, dukkah, crushed nuts, nori strips, broken crackers. Convenience food is almost always uniformly soft, and that is most of why it gets boring.

Bag-marinate. Frozen chicken thighs into a freezer bag with gochujang and honey, or miso and mirin, then straight back in the freezer. Thirty seconds now; an entirely different meal in three weeks.

Buy frozen aromatics. Crushed ginger, garlic, chilli, lemongrass and herbs come in little cubes. They remove the last reason anyone chops anything, and they don't go slimy in the drawer.

Use half the sachet. With instant noodles, most of the fat and nearly all the salt is in the oil and flavour sachets rather than the noodles. Half is plenty, and it leaves room for an egg and a handful of frozen vegetables.

Use the Sakura's delay timer. Load it in the morning, set it for the evening, and dinner cooks itself while you're out. Minimum notice is 70 minutes for most programmes, 130 for slow cook.

Cook once, eat three times. The Sakura's slow cook holds 1.5 litres and the Halo takes over a kilo. Poached chicken, stews, chilli and roasted vegetables keep four days and become wraps, bowls and soups with no further cooking.

Templates ▸ no decisions

Twenty formulas

Shapes rather than recipes. Once these are in your head you stop planning meals and start looking in the freezer and filling in blanks.

  1. Paste plus coconut Any Thai curry paste + a tin of coconut milk + any frozen protein + any frozen veg. One pan, 12–15 minutes, no chopping.
  2. The slow-cook dump Everything into the Sakura on slow cook before you go out: stew, braise, pulses, ragù, onions. Remember it runs at double speed, so 3–4 hours is plenty.
  3. The noodle bowl Frozen udon or instant noodles + a broth base (mentsuyu, miso, laksa paste) + an egg + frozen greens.
  4. The tilted tray Frozen protein and frozen veg into the Halo bowl, dual heat, tilted, 20–25 minutes. It tumbles itself and browns all over.
  5. The rack grill Anything flat — fish, chicken fillet, steak, burgers, halloumi — on the rack, grill mode, level, 15–20 minutes turned once. Fat drips clear.
  6. The steam stack Rice or barley in the Sakura bowl, fish or dumplings and vegetables in the basket above. One machine, whole dinner, one wash.
  7. Tin plus flatbread Tinned butter beans, gigantes or black beans + frozen pitta or Turkish flatbread. Five minutes, no pan.
  8. The one oven tray Frozen protein one end, frozen veg the other, 200°C, 22 minutes, foil-lined so there's nothing to wash.
  9. Jacket plus topping Frozen ready-baked potato in the microwave, or a fresh one 60 minutes level in the Halo, then beans and chipotle, tuna and sweetcorn, or kimchi and cheese.
  10. Pasta plus tin Pasta + one tin of oily fish + one jarred sharp thing (capers, olives, sun-dried tomato). Ten minutes.
  11. The pouch plate Grain pouch + tinned fish + jarred peppers + lemon. No heat at any point.
  12. Roast and dress Frozen veg tilted in the Halo, then tahini-lemon or harissa-yoghurt spooned over. Turns a side into a main.
  13. Egg on top Any leftover, any grain, any roasted veg, plus a fried, jammy or steamed egg. Instantly a meal.
  14. The stew shortcut Tinned pulses + passata + one strong flavour ('nduja, chorizo, harissa, kimchi, anchovy) + something green.
  15. The fermented stew Kimchi or sauerkraut + stock + tofu or sausage + a starch. Sourness does the work seasoning usually would.
  16. Wrap it Wholemeal wrap or pitta + a spread + a protein + something pickled. Assembly only.
  17. Soup plus Carton soup + a drained tin of pulses + crunch on top. Starter becomes dinner.
  18. The conservas plate Two tins of good seafood, bread, pickles, tomatoes. Zero cooking and it still feels like an occasion.
  19. Toastie logic Bread + cheese + one sharp thing (kimchi, gherkin, chutney, jalapeño). The sharp thing is what makes it a meal.

One number ▸ worth watching

The fibre lever

Convenience eating is short on one thing more than any other, and it's the easiest to fix without cooking. Seven changes, none of which adds a step.

Seven no-effort fibre moves

1. Drain a tin of chickpeas, butter beans or cannellini into whatever is already cooking — stew, soup, pasta sauce, a tray of roasting veg.

2. Swap in the pour-and-wait grains: bulgur, freekeh, giant couscous, barley. The Sakura's fast cook setting handles couscous and quinoa; slow cook handles barley and beans.

3. Keep frozen edamame in the freezer door. Two minutes in the microwave, flaky salt, done — a side dish requiring no judgement at all.

4. Buy wholemeal pitta, wraps, pasta and noodles. Identical effort, roughly double the fibre. Soba and buckwheat noodles count too.

5. Frozen veg counts fully. Picked and frozen fast, no washing, no chopping, and it doesn't rot in the drawer.

6. A handful of pearl barley or mochi-mugi (もち麦 · mochimugi · waxy barley) in with white rice. Same water, same button, no change in taste.

7. A spoon of flaxseed or psyllium into porridge, yoghurt or a smoothie — neither tastes of anything, and you already have both.

Three habits that halve the bill

Batch your online orders. Almost every shop above has a free-delivery threshold between £40 and £60. One order a season for shelf-stable goods — roux, pouches, pastes, noodles, sachets — costs less than a supermarket top-up and lasts months.

Use the price guarantee. WASO undertake to beat any UK online price on Japanese groceries, so if Starry Mart or Amazon is cheaper on a roux multipack or a case of coconut milk, it is worth a note in the order — and their frozen delivery is free regardless of distance.

Buy the big size on anything fermented. Miso, gochujang, doenjang, kimchi and chilli crisp all keep for many months refrigerated, and the kilo tub is routinely the price of a supermarket 300 g jar.

When a pouch makes you curious, the three best free resources for going one step further are Just One Cookbook (justonecookbook.com) for Japanese home cooking, Maangchi (maangchi.com) for Korean, and The Woks of Life (thewoksoflife.com) for Chinese — all written for ordinary kitchens, all searchable by the ingredient you just bought.

Learn the script for what you buy. The tables below give the Japanese for each takikomi and furikake type. Photograph the packet you liked, match the characters on the shelf next time, and you stop being dependent on the English label a shop may or may not print.

Four rules that make the whole thing work

Never defrost anything. Everything here goes in frozen. Defrosting is planning, and planning is the part you don't want to do.

Buy the version that needs no knife. Florets not heads, chunks not whole squash, fillets not whole fish, frozen aromatics not fresh. It costs slightly more and removes the reason you'd order a takeaway instead.

Let the right machine do the waiting. Halo tilted for anything dry, Sakura on slow cook or the delay timer for anything wet. Between them almost nothing needs you in the room.

Spend money on flavour, not ingredients. A four-pound jar of paste makes eight dinners. That is the cheapest possible route to food that doesn't bore you, and it requires no skill whatsoever.
